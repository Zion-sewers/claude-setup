# Payments Field Notes: How Money Actually Moves on Card Rails

## What the book actually teaches

Card payments are a three-phase money story, not a three-second one. Nothing moves when the card is swiped: an authorization message travels terminal to acquirer to network to issuer and back in under three seconds, and all it does is place a hold and check a balance. Money moves later, in a second message called clearing (merchant confirms and adjusts, usually end of day, in bulk), then settlement (the network nets amounts between the issuing and acquiring banks, typically next business day, often via the Federal Reserve wire system). On top of this lifecycle sits a fixed fee stack paid by the merchant, a liability machine for disputes that pushes cost onto whoever deviated from the secure path, and a whole ecosystem of companies that exist purely to route, float, or arbitrage the gaps between these phases. The analyst's mental model: every product in the book, from instant wage advances to corporate expense cards, is someone exploiting a timing gap, a fee gap, or a routing choice inside this machinery.

## Core mechanisms

### 1. The two-message lifecycle (authorize, clear, settle)

**How it works.** Message one, authorization: the terminal sends card number, amount, location, merchant type through the acquirer processor to the network (ISO 8583, compact by design), which reads the first six digits (the BIN), finds the issuing bank, and forwards the request. The issuer processor answers five questions: is this our cardholder, is the card active, is there enough money or credit line, is this merchant allowed, and does anything smell like fraud? An approval returns as a hold on the cardholder's available balance, not a transfer. Actual and available balances diverge here. Message two, clearing (also called capture): the merchant submits the day's transactions in bulk, adjusting for tips, voids, and returns. This is the merchant's last chance to fix amounts before money moves. Settlement: the network computes net positions, debits the issuer's settlement account, credits the acquirer's, moves the remainder, and ships settlement files to both processors.

**Illustration.** A coffee purchase authorizes for $4.75 (balance shows $1,000 actual, $995.25 available), then clears at $5.75 after a $1 tip. The pending $4.75 disappears, a $5.75 settled charge appears, and the actual balance drops to $994.25 a day or more after the swipe. Online purchases stay pending longer because clearing typically happens when goods ship.

**Analyst application.** Treat "pending" and "settled" as two different populations in any data. Authorization declines, holds, and reversals are your fraud signal; settled data is your truth for loss and revenue. Risk hides in the seams: auth-clear amount mismatches (is the drift legitimate tip adjustment or transaction stuffing?), voids before clearing (legitimate corrections, or a merchant gaming volume), and the hold window where a cardholder can stack authorizations against one balance. When a fraud case claims "the money was taken instantly," check whether it was only a hold.

### 2. The interchange engine

**How it works.** The merchant pays three fees on every card transaction, and they go to three different places. Interchange goes to the issuing bank (a percentage of the transaction, biggest piece). The network assessment goes to the card network as a toll for the rails. The acquirer fee goes to the merchant's acquirer, often a flat fee billed monthly. Interchange rates come from large network-set rate tables keyed on: card type (credit higher than debit, premium credit like Visa Signature or Mastercard World highest of all), merchant category code or MCC, and how the transaction was run (chip, keyed online, PIN debit, signature debit). In the US, the Durbin Amendment caps interchange for banks over $10 billion in assets at 21 cents plus 0.05% plus a penny for fraud controls, creating a regulated tier and an unregulated tier. Debit cards also run on two sub-rails: signature (dual message, 1-2% interchange) or PIN debit (single message, below 0.5%).

**Illustration.** Walmart programs its terminals to route debit transactions as PIN-less debit without requiring a PIN. That choice sacrifices the PIN's anti-fraud confirmation and accepts a higher chargeback risk it will lose, in exchange for roughly 0.5% lower interchange on enormous volume, which saves millions. Retailers like Macy's and Target go further with private-label cards that never touch the networks, skipping interchange entirely and keeping customer data.

**Analyst application.** MCC is a risk control, not just a pricing knob. Misclassified merchants (deliberate or sloppy) pay wrong interchange and evade category-specific risk rules, which is the raw material of transaction laundering. Watch for: routing anomalies that systematically pick the cheapest rail (legitimate arbitrage by processors like Tabapay, or gaming), and the Durbin split, which is the economic engine behind free banking (more on this in mechanism 7). Any interchange variance analysis should join card tier, MCC, channel, and clearing time, since faster clearing earns better rates.

### 3. The chargeback machine and liability assignment

**How it works.** After settlement, a cardholder who does not recognize a charge can ask the issuing bank for the money back. The bank files the dispute with the network, the cardholder gets provisional credit immediately, and the money is pulled back from the merchant, which then gets 45 days to produce documentation (receipts, proof of EMV chip use, CVV capture, delivery records). If the merchant stays silent, it pays plus a $25-35 network chargeback fee per case, assessed win or lose. Networks want merchant chargeback rates below 1% of transactions, enforced through acquirers, or the merchant loses card acceptance. Liability is assigned by rule, not fairness: if an EMV chip card was swiped by magnetic stripe because the merchant's terminal did not read the chip, the merchant absorbs the loss with no defense. For card-not-present transactions, missing CVV or missing 3D Secure authentication tilts liability toward the merchant. 3D Secure adds a second factor (one-time passcode, then app-based approval) to online checkout, shifting liability back off the merchant.

**Illustration.** A cardholder traveling in Japan sees a late-night online charge at a luxury retailer, calls the issuer, and freezes the card through the bank app while deciding whether the charge is legitimate (it turns out to be a spouse's purchase). The episode shows the real consumer cost of a false fraud flag: card reissue, and updating every recurring biller. About 30% of chargebacks are of this "I think my card was stolen" type.

**Analyst application.** Chargebacks are your richest labeled fraud signal and your dirtiest one: friendly fraud (buyer received goods, disputes anyway) is structurally invisible in the reason code, and the burden of proof always lands on the merchant, so merchant win rates are biased downward. Build separate models and thresholds for fraud-type versus service-type disputes. Monitor chargeback rate as a leading merchant-risk indicator: a merchant drifting toward 1% is either being attacked or has a broken fulfillment process, and specialized high-risk acquirers price this explicitly. The EMV liability shift is the textbook example of networks governing by liability: they did not ban magstripe, they made it expensive to use.

### 4. The card program stack (issuer, processor, program manager, co-brand partner, BIN sponsor)

**How it works.** Only banks can issue on open networks, so every card program is a stack. The card network provides rails and rules. The issuing bank sponsors the BIN (the card's first six digits) and provides compliance oversight; the network will not license a BIN to a non-bank. The issuer processor runs the real-time decision engine: it parses network messages, answers authorize/decline in under three seconds, and exposes APIs so the partner can integrate. The program manager runs day-to-day operations: settlement, fraud management, chargeback handling, KYC, card manufacturing. The co-brand partner is the brand on the card (an airline, a delivery company, an employer). Interchange flows from merchant to issuer and is split: the program manager typically keeps the largest share, then shares revenue downstream with the co-brand partner on a sliding scale tied to volume, and pays fees to the issuing bank and processor.

**Illustration.** A donut delivery startup issues drivers a co-branded debit card that always carries a $0 balance. When a driver swipes at the restaurant, the issuer processor pings the startup's own system in real time, which approves or declines based on its own business rules: exact expected order amount, driver geolocation. The card is funded for the exact swipe amount and returns to $0. This is called just-in-time funding, and it converts the fraud problem from "detect bad spend later" to "decline anything unexpected now."

**Analyst application.** This stack is where fintech fraud controls are actually built. Map every card product to its stack and find who owns the decline decision, because that owner owns the fraud losses operationally even if liability sits elsewhere. JIT-style controls (zero standing balance, per-swipe approval against order data, geo-fencing) are the strongest issuer-side control pattern in the book: study it as the template for any delegated-authorization design. Also note the data trail: the issuer processor reports every swipe, load, and unload to the issuing bank daily, which is the reconciliation layer where program-level fraud (skimming loads, ghost cards) shows up.

### 5. The merchant acceptance ladder (ISO, PayFac, direct acquirer, PSP)

**How it works.** A new merchant can accept cards four ways, each trading speed and simplicity for cost and control. An Independent Sales Organization (ISO) sells acquiring services on behalf of an acquirer bank. A payments facilitator (PayFac) like Square onboards sub-merchants in minutes under its own master merchant account: flat pricing (2.6% plus 10 cents in the book's example), bundled hardware and fraud tools, but the merchant's name appears as a sub-merchant descriptor on cardholder statements, there is a volume ceiling (around $500k a year in the book's case), and settlement is slower (funds settle to the PayFac, which then pushes to the merchant by ACH). A direct merchant acquirer account costs more paperwork and self-managed fraud, but buys interchange-plus pricing that declines with volume, exact descriptor and MCC control, and direct next-day settlement. A payment service provider (PSP) aggregates payment methods (cards, wallets, buy-now-pay-later) behind one integration, with multi-acquirer redundancy underneath.

**Illustration.** A small donut shop starts on a PayFac, grows past the volume where flat pricing is rational, moves to a direct acquirer account with interchange-plus pricing and tiered acquirer fees, then adds a PSP to accept buy-now-pay-later from wholesale grocery buyers that need net-30 terms. Each step matches a stage of the business.

**Analyst application.** PayFac sub-merchant aggregation is a risk concentration point: one master account's underwriting quality determines the fraud exposure of thousands of businesses, and sub-merchant descriptors make transaction laundering harder to spot by brand (though the master descriptor flags the pattern). Watch for merchants whose statement descriptors do not match their apparent MCC, merchants approaching PayFac volume caps (pressure to split or migrate), and settlement-delay arbitrage: anyone holding merchant funds for days is running a float business and a credit business simultaneously. The BNPL pattern (merchant paid next day, buyer pays over 30 days) is a credit product wearing a payments costume; underwrite it as credit.

### 6. Push-to-card (original credit transactions)

**How it works.** Card networks built a transaction type that pushes money onto a debit card the way a merchant refund does, but without any original purchase: the Original Credit Transaction (OCT), productized as Visa Direct and Mastercard Send. The sender's system acts through a merchant acquirer integrated with these services, and funds land on the recipient's debit card in seconds. The only identifiers needed are the card number, which apps read by photographing the card, so the flow needs no bank account or routing numbers.

**Illustration.** An earned-wage-access app advances a retail worker $150 to his debit card at 6 a.m. after a car breakdown, letting him pay the mechanic and get to work. The same rails pay gig drivers their earnings at end of shift instead of weekly direct deposit, usually for a $0.50 to $1.00 fee per cash-out. The technology traces back to a startup that first built business-to-person disbursements (insurance claims paid to auto shops) and was acquired by a network, which then standardized the OCT type.

**Analyst application.** Push-to-card is speed without reversibility. The core risk: anyone who can trigger a push can drain funds instantly, so account takeover on a payout-enabled app is a direct cash-out vector. Watch for burst payouts to newly added cards, payouts followed immediately by card-present or ATM spend, and velocity patterns that look like gig work but pay to cards never seen on the platform. One structural fragility: a lender that pushes to a debit card and expects to pull repayments from the same card loses its collection path the moment the card is frozen or reissued, which is why lenders need an ACH fallback and therefore need the bank account details push-to-card let them skip in the first place.

### 7. ACH: the cheap, slow, overdraft-capable workhorse

**How it works.** The Automated Clearing House moves money bank to bank in batches. The originator (a company, a payroll department) formats debits and credits into a NACHA file and sends it to its bank (the Originating Depository Financial Institution), which routes it through the Federal Reserve to the receiving banks. There are cutoff windows; miss one and you wait for the next. Receiving banks have up to three business days to return a debit that cannot be collected. Fees are charged to the originator monthly, not netted from each transaction, which makes ACH far cheaper than cards at scale. Same-day ACH exists with a morning cutoff and a transaction cap.

**Illustration.** A utility pulls a monthly bill from a customer's checking account. One month the customer has $47.93 and the pull is $73.05: the debit still posts, the account goes negative, the bank charges a $35 overdraft fee, then returns the ACH within the return window. This is a sharp contrast with cards, which decline in real time and do not overdraft. The same customer then pays by credit card over the phone, and the utility ends up paying interchange, network assessment, and acquirer fees on a payment that started as a cheap ACH pull.

**Analyst application.** ACH returns are your canary. Returned debit codes (insufficient funds, account closed, unauthorized) arrive days after the fact, so model them as a lagging but honest signal of account health and fraud. Neo-banks that release direct-deposit funds days early (before the effective date) are floating wages from their own balance sheet; any model of their liquidity or their users' cash flow must account for that float. The cost asymmetry matters: a fraudster who can convert an ACH pull into a card payment (as in the utility example) converts a near-free transaction into one carrying full interchange. And peer-to-peer apps that "move money instantly" are usually doing exactly what the book describes for Venmo: floating funds for days while the real ACH settles behind the curtain.

### 8. The identity gate (KYC) and its failure modes

**How it works.** Before any card or account is issued, the institution must attach a real identity to the user. In the US this usually means name, Social Security number, date of birth, and address, checked against public records, credit data, and fraud/crime watchlists (including OFAC). Banks increasingly do this digitally through KYC providers. A subtle rule: for prepaid and gift cards, KYC is triggered by loading, not spending. A single-load gift card can be bought and spent anonymously; the moment it becomes reloadable, identity must be collected, because reloadable value is a money-laundering vector.

**Illustration.** A small-business lender built on top of a payments platform found that legitimate mom-and-pop merchants were failing automated KYC and being denied loans they could obviously repay. The fix was a waterfall: try the first identity provider, and on failure offer the user another verification path (different document, a selfie match, knowledge-based questions about prior addresses), stepping up friction progressively instead of presenting every document request up front. Conversion recovered without dropping the compliance bar.

**Analyst application.** KYC is a funnel with a measurable drop-off curve, and the shape of that curve is a risk decision, not just a UX one. Every progressive step-up adds friction and loses some good users; every skipped check admits some bad ones. Watch the seam the book names explicitly: the load/spend split on prepaid means spend-side-only monitoring misses the laundering event. "Thin file" applicants (immigrants, the unbanked) fail thin-file checks at higher rates, which creates a systematic bias in any fraud model trained on KYC outcomes, so treat KYC pass/fail as a feature with known measurement error, not as ground truth. Synthetic identity thrives exactly where the book says identity stitching is hardest: real SSN plus fabricated personal details, building a clean file over time.

### 9. The other rails: Zelle, wires, real-time payments

**How it works.** Three non-card ways to move money, each with a different reversibility contract. Zelle moves money between banks as an internal ledger transfer once both parties enroll with a phone number or email; it is effectively instantaneous and irrevocable, with no settlement step. Wire transfers (Fedwire, or CHIPS via The Clearing House) push large amounts bank to bank quickly but expensively, with humans in the loop confirming the transfer, which is why they are used for house closings. Real-time payments (RTP from The Clearing House) are push-only transfers that land in seconds at a fraction of wire cost, but without the instant confirmation the card networks provide.

**Illustration.** A home buyer wires a deposit to a title company, gets a confirmation call from the bank about an hour later, pays a $25 fee, and then calls the title company to confirm receipt. The hour of human confirmation is the entire difference between a wire and RTP.

**Analyst application.** The reversibility contract is the whole risk story. Zelle's irreversibility is what makes authorized push payment scams work: the victim authorizes the transfer, so there is no chargeback machine to appeal to. Any scam-detection model for these rails should score the instruction channel (social engineering happens off-rail) and first-time payee patterns, not transaction anomaly alone. For wires, the human confirmation step is both the control and the attack surface: business email compromise targets exactly that call-back. RTP's lack of delivery confirmation is a reconciliation risk the book flags honestly; do not treat "pushed" as "received" in settlement data.

## Popular take vs sharper argument

**1. "Money moves in three seconds when you swipe."** The common summary of this book is the three-second miracle. The book's actual mechanism says the opposite: at three seconds, nothing moves. A hold is placed, a message is answered, and the money moves a day later through batch files and net settlement. The sharp version: every "instant" payment product in the book is somebody else floating the money. The wage advance app floats against earned wages, the P2P app floats against ACH, the neo-bank floats early direct deposit against the effective date. For a risk analyst, "instant" is a claim about who bears timing risk, never about physics.

**2. "Interchange is a tax the networks charge merchants."** Two corrections. First, interchange goes to the issuing bank, not the network; the network's cut is the smaller assessment fee. Second, the Durbin exemption turned interchange into the business model of free banking: small sponsor banks earn unregulated interchange, and fintech brands built on top of them (the neo-banks in the book) fund zero-fee accounts from it. So the "tax" framing misses that the loudest critics of banking fees are being subsidized by the very interchange they claim to hate. Watch the incentive: any product priced at zero is being paid for by a fee two layers down that the user never sees.

**3. "Better fraud models will fix payment fraud."** The book never says this, but a casual reading invites it, and the sharper argument runs the other way. The system's fraud outcomes are produced by liability assignment, not by detection accuracy. The EMV shift did not happen because chips are smarter; it happened because networks made magnetic-stripe acceptance the merchant's financial problem. Walmart accepts higher chargebacks to save interchange because the math says to. The design variable is who pays, and detection models only operate inside the incentives that assignment creates. A risk analyst who understands liability rules can predict fraud migration better than one with a better classifier.

## The strongest objection to the book's framing

The steelman version of the critique: the book presents card payments as a clean, stable machine and tells the story through founder anecdotes from winners, which is a snapshot, not a law of nature. It is US-centric (Durbin, Reg E, Zelle, and the specific network politics do not transfer cleanly to other markets), it predates real-time settlement rails like FedNow that change the "ACH is slow, cards are fast" dichotomy, and every mechanism it describes is actively gamed in ways the book mostly treats as legitimate cleverness (interchange routing arbitrage, MCC selection, PayFac aggregation) rather than as the attack surface a risk analyst must defend. The founder stories are survivorship narratives; the failed card programs and blown-up PayFacs teach more about risk than the winners do, and they are absent.

Whether it holds: on the storytelling, yes, discount it. On the mechanics, no, the objection does not land. Authorization holds, two-message clearing, the fee stack, the chargeback liability machine, BIN sponsorship, and the push/pull distinction are durable because they are contractual and regulatory, not technical. FedNow changes speed, not who bears loss. The dated parts are the case studies and the instant-payment landscape, not the plumbing. For a risk analyst, treat the book as a correct map of the machine with a 2019 timestamp on the terrain.

## Practitioner's checklist

1. **Pending vs settled reconciliation.** Do your dashboards separate authorization holds from settled funds? If a fraud metric mixes the two, rebuild it.
2. **Auth-clear drift.** For each merchant, what is the distribution of clearing amounts versus authorized amounts? Legitimate tip businesses drift a little; systematic upward drift is stuffing.
3. **MCC integrity.** Sample merchants and verify their category code matches what they actually sell. MCC mismatch is the entry point for both interchange gaming and transaction laundering.
4. **Chargeback rate by merchant, monthly.** Flag any merchant approaching the 1% of transactions threshold. Investigate whether the driver is fraud attack, fulfillment failure, or friendly fraud before the network acts.
5. **Dispute reason-code split.** Separate true fraud disputes from service/not-received disputes. They need different models, different merchant conversations, and different thresholds.
6. **EMV fallback rate.** What share of chip-capable transactions fall back to magstripe, by terminal and by merchant? Every fallback is a liability assignment waiting to happen.
7. **3D Secure coverage.** For card-not-present volume, what share carries step-up authentication, and what is the fraud rate on the uncovered share? That gap is your liability.
8. **Push-to-card velocity.** On any payout product, alert on new-card enrollment followed by immediate cash-out, and on payouts to cards with no prior relationship to the platform. Payout + account takeover = instant loss.
9. **ACH return codes.** Track return rates and code mix by originator. A rising unauthorized-return rate is a compromised or abusive originator; rising insufficient-funds returns is a portfolio health problem.
10. **Early funds float.** If your product releases direct deposits before the effective date, measure the exposure: how much is floated at any moment, and what happens on a mass return event?
11. **KYC waterfall performance.** Measure pass rates and drop-off at each verification step, separately for thin-file populations. A "tighter" KYC policy that silently rejects good immigrants is a bias problem and a growth problem.
12. **Prepaid load monitoring.** Monitor loading events, not just spending, on prepaid and gift products. Reload velocity and round-amount loads are the laundering signal.
13. **Sub-merchant descriptors.** If you touch PayFac traffic, join statement descriptors to actual merchant identity. Descriptor mismatch is where laundering hides in plain sight.
14. **Wire confirmation discipline.** For any wire product, verify the callback control exists and is actually used. Business email compromise attacks the human confirmation step, not the wire itself.
15. **Liability rule changes.** Maintain a living list of who bears loss for each transaction type you handle (chip, magstripe, card-not-present, 3D Secure, push). When a network changes a liability rule, your fraud forecasts change with it; model the rule, not just the data.
